Thanks for the suggestion — this turned out to be a very clean test. Here's what we get.

**Setup.** `same_country` and `same_language` are correlated in one direction in our GS sample:
every same-nationality team-obs also has same_language=1 (0 same-nationality/different-language
teams exist in the data), so Table 3 alone can't separate the two channels. Following your
proposal, I defined three mutually exclusive, exhaustive categories:

- **Same nationality & same language**
- **Different nationality & same language** (e.g. Germany/Austria, UK/Australia/US, Spain/Argentina)
- **Different nationality & different language**

**Descriptives (N and raw win rate):**

| Category | N | Win rate |
|---|---|---|
| Same nationality & same language | 1,534 | 0.4896 |
| Different nationality & same language | 575 | 0.5565 |
| Different nationality & different language | 1,619 | 0.4898 |

**Regression.** The same-nationality-&-same-language dummy is identical to `same_country`, so it
just reproduces Table 3 column 1 exactly (AME=+0.0361, p=0.028) — not worth re-reporting. The
informative test is the different-nationality/same-language dummy, entered on its own (same
controls/FE/clustering as Table 3), against the full remaining sample:

| Variable | AME | SE | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Different nationality & same language (vs. everyone else) | +0.0156 | 0.0215 | 0.467 |

N = 3,728 team-obs, Pseudo-R² = 0.095.

**Bottom line:** the performance association does **not** survive for different-nationality/
same-language partnerships (p = 0.467) — only the same-nationality category (Table 3 col. 1,
p = 0.028) is significant. So this points *against* a purely communication/language-driven
mechanism and is more consistent with nationality-specific factors (shared sporting
institutions, geographic proximity, prior junior-circuit exposure) driving the original result,
rather than shared language per se.

One robustness note: I also ran a version where both dummies are entered *jointly* in the same
regression (reference category = different-nationality/different-language only, rather than
"everyone else"). That version gives a somewhat less clear-cut null (AME=+0.0353, p=0.119) since
excluding the same-nationality teams from the reference group tightens the comparison. Both cuts
agree on the qualitative conclusion — the diff-nationality/same-language effect is not
significant — but I wanted to flag that the exact p-value is somewhat sensitive to how the
reference group is defined, in case that matters for how you'd want to frame it.
